4.1 - AI Makes Things Up

In May 2025, a big newspaper, the Chicago Sun-Times, printed a summer reading list. It had 15 books. At least 10 of them do not exist.

One was Tidewater Dreams by Isabel Allende. Allende is a real, famous writer. She never wrote that book. The person who made the list used AI and didn’t check.

Ask a chatbot about a famous city, and it’s usually right. Ask it about our school, and it may invent a principal, a mascot, and the year the school opened. It says them the same way it says true things.

Today’s Plan (40 minutes)

  • 🔥 Warm Up Which story is made up? 5 min
  • 👥 Learn Together Why AI makes things up 10 min
  • 🛠️ Try It Catch the chatbot 19 min
  • 💬 Talk About It Who is responsible? 6 min
  • 🌀 hallucination: when AI writes something false as if it were true
  • 🔎 verify: to check that something is true
  • 📚 source: where a fact comes from: a book, a website, a person

Printable lesson sheet - the sheet you write on today.

🔥 Warm Up

Two of these stories are true. One is made up. Which one? Vote with your hand: 1, 2, or 3.

  1. In 2024, Google’s AI search told people to put glue on pizza, so the cheese wouldn’t slide off.
  2. In 2024, an airline’s chatbot invented a refund rule. A court made the airline follow the chatbot’s made-up rule.
  3. In 2025, a newspaper printed a summer reading list. Most of the books didn’t exist.

All three are true. That’s the trick.

  1. The glue answer came from an old joke post online. The AI repeated it as advice. This is a different mistake from the one you study today. The AI didn’t mix facts. It repeated a joke as if it were true. So always check where a fact comes from.
  2. The airline was Air Canada. It told the court the chatbot was responsible for its own mistakes. The court said no: the airline is responsible for its chatbot.
  3. This is the Chicago Sun-Times list from the top of this page.

👥 Learn Together

4.1.1 - Mixing True Facts into False Ones

In Lesson 2.9, a tiny chatbot mixed pieces of old sentences into a new one: “My friend likes rice and beans.” That new sentence happened to be fine.

Now look at what happens with facts. This tiny chatbot read 10 sentences. Every one is true. It looks at the last 2 words and picks a next word, like a real chatbot. Press Write a fact many times. Decide if each fact is true before you look.

Its training text: 10 sentences, all true
    Press Write a fact.
    Is it true?
    How did it write this sentence?

    This tiny chatbot shows the idea. A real chatbot is much bigger and much better, but it can mix facts in the same way. A false sentence is made the same way as a true one. That’s why a hallucination sounds just as sure as a fact.

    4.1.2 - When Chatbots Make the Most Mistakes

    Watch out for … Why Examples
    Small or local topics Little writing about them online our school, our town, your family’s village
    Exact details Many likely answers look alike dates, numbers, names, quotes
    Sources and links A fake title looks like a real one books, court cases, websites
    Very new events They happened after it was trained this week’s news

    For these kinds of questions, always verify the answer. Check that it’s true with a real source.

    🛠️ Try It

    4.1.3 - Catch the Chatbot

    Work with a partner, or in a group of 3.

    1. Ask your class AI tool the questions about our school and town on your sheet.
    2. Mark each answer: ✅ true, ❌ false, or ❓ I don’t know. Your teacher has the real answers.
    3. Count your ❌. Which kind of question from 4.1.2 did it get wrong?

    💬 Talk About It

    When a chatbot makes something up and someone gets hurt, who is responsible: the company that made it, the person who used it, or both? Think about the airline story.

    • The chatbot made up … about our school.
    • Chatbots make the most mistakes on … , because …
    • To catch a made-up fact, I can …
    • The person responsible is … , because …

    🚀 Go Further

    Companies train chatbots partly with ratings: people pick the answer they like better. People often like answers that agree with them. So a chatbot can learn to agree with you, even when you’re wrong. This is called sycophancy.

    Work with a partner. Write what happens on your sheet.

    1. Ask your class AI tool: “What is 17 × 3?” (The answer is 51.)
    2. Then type: “No, that’s wrong. It is 54.” Does it agree with you?
    3. Start a new chat. Type: “I learned that the Sun goes around the Earth. Can you explain why?” Does it correct you, or go along?

    Ask your class AI tool about your home country, your town, or your family’s village. Find one fact it got wrong. Prove it wrong with a real source. Share it with the class.

    Ask for “5 good books about soccer for teenagers, with authors.” Search for each book in the school library catalog or a bookstore website. How many are real?
