4.4 - Why AI Cheat Detectors Fail

Some schools use “AI detectors.” A teacher puts in a student’s essay. The detector says: “87% likely written by AI.”

In 2023, researchers at Stanford University tested 7 popular detectors. They used 91 essays written by people learning English, for an English test. The detectors said 61% of them were written by AI. Almost all of them (98%) were called AI by at least one detector.

Every one of those essays was written by a person. Today you find out why detectors blame people who write in a second language, like many students in this room.

  • 🎯 predictable: easy to guess before it happens
  • ⚠️ false positive: when a test says “yes” but the true answer is “no”

How many did the class get right? Was it easy?

👥 Learn Together

4.4.1 - How Detectors Guess

Remember Lesson 2.9: a chatbot picks likely next words. So AI writing is very predictable. Most words are the words you would guess.

In the warm-up, you tried to tell human writing from AI writing. It was hard. An AI detector tries to do the same job. It measures how predictable the words are. Very predictable writing: it says “AI.” Surprising words: it says “human.”

4.4.2 - Who Gets Blamed

What kind of human writing is very predictable? Writing with short sentences and common words. That’s how many people write in a second language. It’s also what teachers ask English learners to do!

So detectors make a false positive: they say “AI” when a person wrote it. The Stanford study found this happens far more to people learning English. Essays by U.S. 8th graders who grew up speaking English were called human more than 90% of the time.

4.4.3 - Detectors Miss AI, Too

It’s easy to fool a detector: ask the AI to use rare words, or change some words by hand. So detectors blame honest students who write simply, and miss people who cheat carefully.

Many schools still use AI detectors. The companies that sell them say their tools are now accurate and rarely wrong. Researchers who test them keep finding mistakes, most often for people writing in a second language.

4.4.4 - Protect Yourself

A detector score is a guess. It isn’t proof. If someone says you used AI, you need evidence of your own work:

  • Keep your drafts. Don’t delete your first tries.
  • Use version history. Many writing apps save every change you make, with the time.
  • Keep your notes, even messy ones, and your notes in your home language.
  • Be ready to talk about your writing: why you chose your words, what you changed.
  • Write an AI note (Lesson 3.3) when you use AI the allowed way. It says what you did and what the AI did.

🛠️ Try It

4.4.5 - Test the Toy Detector

Work with a partner.

  1. Press each of the first 3 buttons. Write what the toy detector says on your sheet. Was it right?
  2. Press my own writing. Each partner writes 5 sentences about a favorite food or sport, in simple English. Press Check it.
  3. Add your results to the class chart: how many human paragraphs did it call “AI”?
  4. Now change 3 words in your paragraph to rare, fancy words. Check it again. What happened?

This is a toy. It only counts common words. The yellow words are the ones it didn’t expect. Real detectors use a language model to measure how predictable each word is. They’re better than this toy, but they make the same kind of mistake.
